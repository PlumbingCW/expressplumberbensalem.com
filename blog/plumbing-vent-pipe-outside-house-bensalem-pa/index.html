Plumbing Vent Pipe Outside the House: A Bensalem, PA Homeowner Guide

By the Bensalem Express Plumbing team. Published November 14, 2026. Updated November 14, 2026.

The pipe poking through your roof is a plumbing vent. It lets air into the drain system so water flows freely, and it carries sewer gas up and away from the house. When a vent is blocked, drains gurgle, toilets bubble and odors seep into the home.

This guide explains what vents do, what blocks them and what you can safely check. It also covers the signs that a vent problem is really a drain problem.

What a vent pipe does

Drain water needs air behind it to move, the same way a bottle empties smoothly only when air can enter. A vent pipe supplies that air. Without it, water leaving one fixture can pull the water seal out of the trap at another, and sewer gas can enter the room.

Every fixture needs venting, and the vents usually join into one stack that exits through the roof. The pipe you see outside is the end of that system. It is open at the top by design, so rain and debris can enter and, in winter, frost can close it. Vent sizing and termination height are set by International Plumbing Code (IPC) standards, which is why random patches on a vent are risky.

Vent trouble looks like drain trouble, so check both.

What blocks a roof vent

These are the usual culprits.

  • ✓Leaves, bird nests and other debris dropped into the open pipe.
  • ✓Frost that closes the opening in extreme cold.
  • ✓A collapsed or crushed section inside the wall or attic.
  • ✓A vent that was never tied in correctly during a remodel.
  • ✓A vent cap that was added and traps debris.

What you can check safely

From the ground, look for the vent pipe on the roof. Make sure it is open and clear. Do not climb on the roof in wet or icy weather, and do not lean a ladder against a pipe. If you cannot see the opening or reach it safely, leave it to a plumber.

Inside, run water in the fixtures one at a time. If one fixture gurgles and the others do not, the vent for that fixture or its branch is the likely problem. If every fixture is slow, the main drain is more likely. A plumber can use a camera or a smoke test to find the path.

Local note: permits and venting rules

Venting is covered by the plumbing code. Bensalem Township has opted into Pennsylvania's Uniform Construction Code, and its Building and Planning office handles permits and inspections. We do not paraphrase specific code sections here. Ask the office at 215-633-3644 what applies to your project.

Venting problems often show up after a remodel, when a new fixture was tied into an old line. Neighbors in Levittown and Langhorne who have finished a basement bath ask the same question. If you are planning one, see plumbing installation.

When a vent problem needs a plumber

A plumber can clear a blocked vent from the roof with a cable or a hose, or from inside the attic. If the vent has collapsed or was never connected properly, the fix is a repair. A camera helps confirm where the problem is.

Sewer gas is more than an odor. It contains gases that can be harmful in a closed space. If you smell it strongly and cannot find the source, ventilate the room and call. Do not ignore a persistent smell.

Vent types you may find in a house

Not every fixture has its own pipe to the roof. A vent can join others in the attic, and some fixtures use air admittance valves, which let air in and keep gas out. These are common in remodels where a roof vent is hard to add.

Air admittance valves are allowed in some places and not in others, and they wear out. If a valve sticks, the drain gurgles. Ask the township's Building and Planning office what is permitted before you add one.

Questions for a plumber about vents

These help you understand what you have.

  • ✓Where does each vent go, and how does it reach the roof?
  • ✓Are there any air admittance valves, and where?
  • ✓Was any venting changed during past remodels?
  • ✓Is the roof termination clear and the right height?
  • ✓Could a camera or a smoke test confirm the path?
